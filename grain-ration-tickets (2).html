Memories of Grain Ration Tickets and the Lessons of History

A small ration ticket recalls a family’s struggle and the lessons of an era.

Qi Guo · July 31, 2026 · San Francisco

A few days ago, while sorting through old books, a small, yellowed slip of paper fell from between the pages. I picked it up and looked closely — it was a grain ration ticket from decades ago. That tiny piece of paper had slept quietly for so many years; now, seeing it again, a long‑sealed chapter of life resurfaced before my eyes.

During the era of state monopoly on grain procurement and distribution, food was rationed strictly by headcount. To buy rice or flour, one needed tickets. The amount of grain you could buy depended entirely on how many tickets you had — even money could not guarantee food.

My family had many children, and our grain quota was always tight. We barely scraped by. How to stretch the limited ration so the family would not run out of food before the end of the month — that burden fell mostly on my mother.

At the start of each month, my mother would sit under the dim lamp, spreading the grain tickets across the table and calculating carefully. How much to eat each day, how much should remain by mid‑month, how much must be saved for the end — she computed everything with precision. It wasn’t that she was naturally frugal; it was that even a small miscalculation could leave the family without grain, and the children hungry.

Cooking, too, could not be done freely. One extra handful of rice today meant one fewer handful later. Eating a little more now might mean one less meal at month’s end. What my mother guarded was not merely a few slips of paper, but the livelihood of the entire family. This “calculating from the first day to the last” way of living continued year after year, for more than a decade.

Looking back now, this was not only my family’s story — it was the shared experience of millions of ordinary households. Countless mothers made such painstaking calculations; countless children grew up never quite full. People endured quietly, rarely complained, and only hoped their families could get through each month safely.

The ancients said: “Reading brings understanding; living through the world reveals change.” This grain ticket, preserved by chance, seems ordinary, yet it is a living page of history. It taught me that when production is overly constrained and markets lose vitality, shortages inevitably follow. And when systems become rigid, the ones who suffer most are the countless households whose daily meals depend on stability.

Seeing this obsolete grain ticket today, I feel even more deeply how hard‑won our era of reform, openness, and material abundance truly is. Today’s markets are full, choices are plentiful, and young people may find it hard to imagine that a tiny slip of paper once determined whether a family could eat enough.

History should not be merely dates and terms on paper — it should also remember the warmth and hunger of ordinary people. Governance must ultimately respect economic laws, develop production, and safeguard livelihoods. When granaries are full, the people feel secure; when families have no worries about food and clothing, society gains its foundation of stability.

A grain ticket is thin, yet its meaning is weighty. It records my mother’s toil and preserves the imprint of a generation’s life. May future generations not forget those difficult years, take history as a mirror, cherish today’s abundance, and allow every household to live in warmth and peace.
