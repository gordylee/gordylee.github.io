Memories of My Childhood in Guangzhou

The Pearl River still flows toward the sea, but the Guangzhou of my childhood now lives in memory.

Qi Guo · July 26, 2026 · San Francisco

An old classmate recently shared a color film of Guangzhou, shot by an American cameraman in 1949. As I watched, the city of more than eighty years ago came vividly back to life. The Pearl River flowed majestically. The Customs House Clock Tower rose proudly over the waterfront. Yide Road bustled with shops, and the windows of watchmakers glittered with clocks and timepieces.

It was not a movie, nor a scene recreated by historians. It was the Guangzhou of my childhood—the city I once knew with my own eyes. Though the decades have slipped away, those memories remain as clear as yesterday.

I attended Jieguanting Primary School on Yonghan Road, today’s Beijing Road, then known as Guangzhou Municipal No. 1 Primary School. One of my closest classmates was the son of the owner of the famous Li Zhan Ji Watch Shop. In those days, many shopkeepers lived above or behind their stores. I often visited his home, listening to the gentle ticking of countless clocks while watching tiny gears turn with quiet precision. Even now, I can still hear those familiar chimes, echoing from a Guangzhou that once flourished as one of South China’s great commercial centers.

During the War of Resistance against Japanese Aggression, Guangzhou suffered repeated air raids. Government offices were moved north to Shaoguan, and when I was only three or four years old, my parents took me there. Yet even Shaoguan was not beyond the reach of Japanese bombers. Whenever the air-raid sirens sounded, adults hurriedly gathered their children and rushed into underground shelters. Although I was only a small child, the piercing sirens, the anxious faces, and the hurried footsteps have never faded from my memory.

After the war ended, Japanese prisoners of war could still be seen sweeping the streets of Guangzhou. They walked with lowered heads, silently doing their assigned work. As a child who had lived through bombings and constant air-raid alarms, I knew Japan only as the enemy. One day, after finishing a banana, I threw the peel toward one of the prisoners. Looking back today, I do not see cruelty in that childish act. I see the instinctive response of a young boy whose earliest memories had been shaped by war.

Like many boys, I was adventurous. Without telling my parents, I secretly went swimming near Tianzi Wharf on the Pearl River. When my father found out, I received a severe scolding. At the time I felt unfairly treated, but now I understand that behind his sternness was a father’s deep love and concern. The waters of the Pearl River carried not only my laughter but also some of the happiest days of my youth.

My mother often took me shopping along Yide Road, one of Guangzhou’s busiest commercial streets. Seafood, dried goods, groceries, fruits, and traditional snacks filled every corner. I loved following her, not only because the streets were lively, but because merchants were remarkably generous. Customers were encouraged to sample before buying. Peanuts, raisins, red dates—I happily tasted my way from one shop to another and often returned home with my stomach already half full. She also shopped frequently at Taipingsha Market, whose lively voices and cheerful bargaining still echo in my memory.

Life after the war was far from easy. Every month, American relief supplies were distributed at Central Park, behind the city government building. My mother often took me there to collect powdered milk provided through American aid, which she brought home to feed my younger brother. These may seem like small details in history, but to our family they were precious reminders of hope during difficult times.

My mother’s relatives owned a fabric store on Gaodi Street, another famous commercial district in old Guangzhou. Rolls of silk and colorful fabrics filled the shop, while merchants and customers came and went throughout the day. As a child, I was fascinated by the brilliant colors and the lively atmosphere, never imagining that these ordinary scenes would one day become treasured memories.

School life also left a deep impression on me. Our school motto was “Propriety, Righteousness, Integrity, and Honor.” Every Monday morning, the entire school gathered to pay tribute before the portrait of Dr. Sun Yat-sen, the Father of Modern China. Though I was too young to join the Scouts, the values of honesty, self-discipline, and love of country quietly became part of my character, guiding me throughout my life.

The Pearl River still flows toward the sea, and the old clock tower still stands. Yet the Guangzhou of my childhood now lives only in memory.

A clock tower, a watch shop, the Pearl River, a loving mother, and a strict but caring father—together they shaped not only my childhood but also the spirit of an era. Time has carried me far from my homeland, yet the city of my youth remains forever alive in my heart.

San Francisco
